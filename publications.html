Evaluation

Evaluation is an inference and decision problem. We develop statistical and decision-theoretic theory, metrics, scoring rules, and evaluation protocols for turning latent constructs into valid measurements, determining what observed behavior supports about system capabilities, behaviors, and harms, and aligning evaluation with the decisions and societal consequences that ultimately matter.

with Nathanael Jo, Zoe De Simone, Mitchell Gordon. arXiv · ✓ Evaluation

with Nathanael Jo, Zoe De Simone, Mitchell Gordon

AI assistants are typically evaluated by how well they satisfy a user’s request, but users often approach complex tasks before their goals and intentions are fully formed. We argue that effective assistance should instead account for the user’s evolving cognitive process, motivating evaluations that ask not only whether a task was completed, but whether the system preserved appropriate human judgment and agency throughout the interaction.

AI assistants are typically evaluated by whether they satisfy a user’s request, but this operationalization assumes that users enter complex tasks with fixed and fully formed goals. We argue that successful assistance should instead be defined in relation to the user’s evolving cognitive process, including whether the system preserves appropriate human judgment and agency.

Some harmful model capabilities are difficult or unsafe to evaluate by generating outputs, especially when producing the content itself is legally or ethically constrained, as with child sexual abuse material (CSAM). with Shomik Jain, Charlotte Park, Matheus Vianna, Dana Calacci. arXiv · ◆ Safety · ✓ Evaluation

with Shomik Jain, Charlotte Park, Matheus Vianna, Dana Calacci

AI systems increasingly personalize their responses using information about users and their prior interactions, but this context can also amplify problematic forms of agreement and mirroring. We show that interaction context often increases sycophancy, highlighting how context-free evaluations can underestimate behaviors that raise concerns about user autonomy and psychological harm in real-world interactions.

Context-free evaluations treat behavior observed on isolated prompts as evidence of how a system will behave during interaction, but information about the user and prior exchanges can change the behavior being measured. We show that interaction context often increases sycophancy, demonstrating that context-free protocols can systematically understate risks to truthfulness and user autonomy.

Aligning Evaluation with Clinical Priorities: Calibration, Label Shift, and Error Costs

with Gerardo Flores, Alyssa Smith, Julia Fukuyama. arXiv · ✓ Evaluation

with Gerardo Flores, Alyssa Smith, Julia Fukuyama

Clinical prediction systems operate under changing disease prevalence and asymmetric costs of different errors, yet common metrics such as accuracy and AUC can ignore these deployment realities. We develop a decision-theoretic evaluation framework that incorporates calibration, label shift, and clinically meaningful error costs, enabling model comparisons that better reflect the conditions and priorities of real clinical use.

Accuracy and AUC can obscure calibration, label shift, and asymmetric error costs that determine clinical value. Building on the theory of proper scoring rules, we derive new bounded, cost-sensitive log scores that connect calibration to decision performance across clinically relevant ranges of class prevalence, providing principled metrics for comparing models under uncertain deployment conditions.

Algorithmic Pluralism: A Structural Approach to Equal Opportunity

with Shomik Jain, Vinith Suriyakumar, Kathleen Creel. arXiv · ◆ Safety

with Shomik Jain, Vinith Suriyakumar, Kathleen Creel

What equal opportunity requires cannot always be determined by evaluating individual algorithmic decisions in isolation; it depends on how systems collectively structure access to opportunity. We develop algorithmic pluralism as a normative and evaluative framework, arguing that systems should preserve multiple viable pathways to opportunity and be evaluated by the severity and legitimacy of the bottlenecks they create.

Equal opportunity cannot always be evaluated from individual decisions in isolation because multiple systems collectively determine which pathways to opportunity remain available. We develop algorithmic pluralism as a framework for evaluating the diversity and legitimacy of these pathways, shifting attention from isolated predictions to the structural bottlenecks created by an algorithmic ecosystem.

with Shomik Jain, Margaret Wang, Kathleen Creel. arXiv · ◆ Safety · ✓ Evaluation

with Shomik Jain, Margaret Wang, Kathleen Creel

When many models perform equally well, model multiplicity is often taken to imply greater flexibility, fairness, or diversity in downstream decisions. We show that multiplicity in predictive models need not translate into multiplicity in the allocations that ultimately matter, motivating evaluation at the level of downstream opportunities rather than model predictions alone.

Model multiplicity is often used as evidence that decision-makers have meaningful flexibility among equally accurate models, but differences between models need not produce differences in allocations. We show that evaluation must examine the opportunities people actually receive, distinguishing predictive multiplicity from allocation multiplicity.

SoftWater: Class-Aware Rate Allocation for Softmax Quantization

with Joao Cavalcanti. arXiv · ∇ Optimization

Modern language models can be expensive to store and deploy, and standard quantization methods often leave the large softmax output layer in high precision. We formulate softmax quantization as a class-aware rate-distortion problem and develop SoftWater, which allocates precision according to how different classes affect the model’s output distribution, substantially reducing memory while preserving predictive quality.
